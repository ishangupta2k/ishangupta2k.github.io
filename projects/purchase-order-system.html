Purchase Order System: Business Analysis Case Study

What this is: a business-analysis case study for a production purchase-order system used daily across two retail locations. It documents the problem, stakeholders, current and future state, requirements, business rules, data mapping, user stories, test coverage, rollout, and measured results.

1. Business problem and scope

Problem statement

Daily vendor ordering across two retail locations was a manual spreadsheet process consuming roughly 6 hours per day of staff time. An order required a person to open a sales export, an inventory export, a vendor list, and an item master, then cross-reference case quantities and pack-to-single relationships by hand for each of roughly 20 vendors. The work was repetitive but not mechanical: suggested quantities still had to reflect how ordering actually worked in the store, so it could not simply be handed to a formula without encoding the store's real rules first.

Business impact of the current state

  • Staff hours consumed by data assembly rather than customer-facing work.
  • Ordering errors from manual cross-referencing, particularly on items sold both as singles and as multi-packs.
  • Inconsistent ordering between the two locations and between different people doing the task.
  • No repeatable record of why a given quantity was ordered.

In scope

  • Daily purchase-order generation for 2 locations, roughly 20 vendors, and 5,000+ SKUs.
  • Demand calculation from point-of-sale sales and inventory exports.
  • Per-vendor ordering configuration.
  • Pack and single (linked scan code) relationships.
  • A human review and adjustment step before any order is placed.
  • Support for two different export formats as the business migrated point-of-sale platforms.

Out of scope

  • Automatic order submission to vendors. Final ordering decisions stay with a person by design (see BR-14).
  • Price, cost, or margin optimization.
  • Physical inventory counting or cycle counts.
  • Vendor contract or payment terms.

Key stakeholder conflict and resolution

Staff wanted the system to just place the orders. The owner wanted to keep spend under human control. Resolution: the system generates a complete, ready-to-send order plus a visible calculation detail sheet and an editable final-order column. Automation handles the assembly; the person keeps the decision. This became BR-14 and drove the review-screen requirement (FR-08).

3. Current state process

  1. Staff export a sales report from the point-of-sale system.
  2. Staff export a current inventory report.
  3. Staff open the vendor list and the item master to find which items belong to which vendor and what the case quantity is.
  4. For each vendor, staff scan for items that look low, estimate recent demand from memory or by reading the sales report, and subtract what is on the shelf.
  5. Staff manually handle items sold as both singles and multi-packs, deciding which one to order and converting between them.
  6. Staff assemble the order in a spreadsheet and send it to the vendor.
Pain points identified: steps 3 through 5 are where the time and the errors concentrate. Step 5 is the single largest source of wrong orders, because the same physical product can appear under two different scan codes with different units.

4. Future state process

  1. Sales and inventory exports land in a watched location on a schedule.
  2. The engine normalizes scan codes, determines current inventory per item, and totals demand over a defined recent sales window.
  3. Linked single sales and inventory roll into the orderable pack using the conversion ratio.
  4. The order formula produces a suggested case quantity per item using per-vendor settings.
  5. The system writes a vendor workbook per vendor containing the suggested order, a calculation detail sheet, and an editable final-order column.
  6. Staff review, adjust where needed, and send.

Net change: staff move from assembling orders to reviewing them. The judgment step is preserved; the data assembly is not.

6. Business rules catalog

These are the operative rules, with their production default values.

Demand and order sizing

IDRuleValue / logic
BR-01Demand is measured over a recent sales window, not all historyDefault 14 days, configurable per vendor
BR-02The sales window ends on the most recent date present in the file, not today's datePrevents a stale export from understating demand
BR-03Daily sales rateunits sold in window / window days
BR-04Target coverage: how many days of supply an order should reachDefault 21 days, configurable per vendor
BR-05Existing inventory is credited against need at a discount, not at face valueDefault credit factor 0.8
BR-06Needed unitsdaily rate × target days − (credit factor × inventory)
BR-07Suggested cases, always rounded up to a whole caseceil(needed units / case quantity)
BR-08If needed units is zero or negative, order nothingSuggested cases = 0

The formula in one line:

dailyRate      = salesUsed / salesWindowDays
neededUnits    = dailyRate * targetDays - inventoryCredit * max(0, inventory)
suggestedCases = neededUnits > 0 ? ceil(neededUnits / caseQty) : 0

Guards and exclusions

IDRuleReason
BR-09Items with less than 1 unit sold in the window are not orderedNo demand signal; avoids restocking dead items automatically
BR-10Items with a missing or zero case quantity are not orderedThe order cannot be sized; requires master-data correction instead of a guess
BR-11Negative inventory is treated as zero, never as extra needNegative counts are a data error, not real demand
BR-12Only items flagged active and orderable appear on an orderDiscontinued items and non-orderable singles are excluded
BR-13Current inventory for an item is taken from its most recent dated rowExports contain multiple rows per item over time
BR-14The system never places an order; it prepares one for reviewOwner retains control of spend

Pack and single (linked scan code) rules

IDRuleLogic
BR-15Sales of a linked single roll into the orderable pack, converted by ratiolinkedSales / conversionRatio added to pack demand
BR-16Total demand for a pack is its own sales plus converted linked salesdirectSales + linkedSales/ratio
BR-17Inventory precedence: use the pack's own inventory when it exists and is above zeroOtherwise fall back to converted linked inventory
BR-18Linked inventory fallback conversionlinkedInventory / conversionRatio
Why BR-15 to BR-18 exist: the same physical product can be sold as a single and ordered as a pack, under two different scan codes. Without these rules the system would either miss demand entirely (single sales invisible to the pack) or double-order (both codes ordered separately). This was the largest source of manual error in the current-state process.

Data normalization

IDRuleLogic
BR-19Scan codes are normalized before any matchingStrip spreadsheet text wrappers, remove non-digits, strip leading zeros
BR-20An all-zero scan code normalizes to 0 rather than emptyPreserves a real, if unusual, code
BR-21Dates are accepted as spreadsheet date values, ISO strings, US slash format, or serial numbersExports are inconsistent across platforms
BR-22Dates are compared on their calendar date in UTCPrevents timezone drift from shifting an item in or out of the sales window

Planning and exception rules

IDRuleDefinition
BR-23Stockout riskinventory ≤ 0 and sales > 0 in the window
BR-24Dead stock / no-sales inventoryinventory > 0 and sales = 0 in the window
BR-25Days of supplyinventory / (sales / window days)
BR-26High stockdays of supply > 30

8. User stories and acceptance criteria

US-01 — Generate a vendor order from exports

As store staff, I want a purchase order generated per vendor from the daily exports, so that I do not assemble orders by hand.

  • Given valid sales and inventory exports, when the run completes, then one order is produced per vendor with items, suggested cases, and a total.
  • Given an item belonging to no vendor, then it does not appear on any order.

US-02 — Size orders from recent demand

As store staff, I want quantities based on recent sales rather than a fixed reorder point, so that orders track actual movement.

  • Given 28 units sold in a 14-day window, a case quantity of 4, target 21 days, and credit 0.8, when inventory is 0, then suggested cases is 11.
  • Given the same item with 10 units on hand, then suggested cases drops to 9.

US-03 — Credit existing inventory

As the owner, I want stock on hand subtracted from need at a discount, so that we neither over-order nor cut it too close.

  • Given inventory above zero, then needed units are reduced by 0.8 × inventory.
  • Given the credit is configurable per vendor, then changing it changes only that vendor's orders.

US-04 — Order in whole cases

As store staff, I want quantities in whole cases, so that the order is placeable with the vendor.

  • Given needed units of 41 and a case quantity of 4, then suggested cases is 11, not 10.25.
  • Given needed units at or below zero, then suggested cases is 0.

US-05 — Roll single sales into the pack

As store staff, I want sales of a loose single counted toward the pack we actually order, so that demand is not invisible.

  • Given a pack with 4 units sold and a linked single with 36 units sold at a ratio of 12, then demand used for the pack is 7.
  • Given the single is flagged non-orderable, then it does not appear as its own order line.

US-06 — Fall back to linked inventory

As store staff, I want the single's shelf stock counted when the pack shows zero, so that we do not over-order a product we already have.

  • Given pack inventory of 0 and linked single inventory of 24 at a ratio of 12, then inventory used for the pack is 2.
  • Given pack inventory above zero, then the pack's own inventory is used and the linked fallback is ignored.

US-07 — Configure ordering per vendor

As the store manager, I want window, target days, and inventory credit set per vendor, so that fast and slow vendors are ordered differently.

  • Given a vendor set to a 7-day window and 10 target days, and 14 units sold with a case quantity of 2, then suggested cases is 10.
  • Given no vendor override, then the defaults of 14 days, 21 target days, and 0.8 credit apply.

US-08 — Reject a bad upload with a clear reason

As store staff, I want to be told exactly what is wrong with a file, so that I can fix the export instead of guessing.

  • Given a workbook missing the quantity column, then the run fails naming the accepted alternatives: Current Qty or In Stock or Current Inventory.
  • Given a workbook missing a required sheet, then the message names both required sheets.
  • Given a sheet with headers but no data rows, then the run fails stating the sheet has no data rows.

US-09 — Match scan codes reliably

As store staff, I want items matched despite formatting differences between exports, so that items are not silently dropped.

  • Given the same product appears as 000123, 123, and a spreadsheet text-wrapped ="123", then all three match one item.
  • Given a code of 0000, then it normalizes to 0 and is retained.

US-10 — Treat negative inventory as zero

As store staff, I want negative counts ignored rather than inflating an order, so that a data error does not become an over-order.

  • Given inventory of −15, then the inventory credit applied is 0.
  • Given inventory of −15, then the item is not double-counted as extra demand.

US-11 — Exclude items that should not be ordered

As the store manager, I want discontinued and non-orderable items kept off orders, so that we stop buying things we dropped.

  • Given an item flagged inactive, then it never appears on an order.
  • Given an item flagged non-orderable, then it never appears even when it has sales.

US-12 — Review and adjust before sending

As store staff, I want to change any quantity before the order goes out, so that judgment and vendor conversations still count.

  • Given a generated order, then every line has an editable final-order value.
  • Given an edited quantity, then the order total recomputes immediately.
  • Given the system runs, then no order is transmitted to a vendor automatically.

US-13 — See why a quantity was suggested

As store staff, I want the math behind each line visible, so that I can check a number I disagree with.

  • Given any order line, then the calculation detail shows units sold in the window, daily rate, target days, inventory used, case quantity, and suggested cases.
  • Given a linked item, then the detail shows the rolled-up linked contribution.

US-14 — Flag exceptions for planning

As the store manager, I want stockout risk and dead stock surfaced, so that I can act before a customer notices.

  • Given inventory at or below 0 with sales above 0, then the item is flagged stockout risk.
  • Given inventory above 0 with no sales in the window, then the item is flagged no-sales inventory.
  • Given days of supply above 30, then the item is flagged high stock.

US-15 — Export the reviewed order

As store staff, I want the final order exported in a vendor-ready format, so that I can send it without retyping.

  • Given a reviewed order, then it exports to CSV and to a vendor workbook.
  • Given adjustments were made, then the export reflects the adjusted values, not the original suggestions.

9. Test approach and results

Approach

The order rules were deliberately built to be testable without the spreadsheet layer (NFR-05), so a rule change can be verified in seconds rather than by a full production run. Testing ran at three levels:

  1. Automated rule tests on the formula, rollup, parsing, validation, and analytics rules.
  2. Comparison against staff-prepared orders during acceptance: generated orders were reviewed against what staff would have ordered manually, and disagreements were traced back to a rule and either corrected or accepted with a reason.
  3. Live operation on real daily exports before staff were asked to rely on the output.

10. Known issues and accepted design decisions

Documented deliberately, because each one is a real limit a reviewer should know about.

IDIssue or decisionStatusReasoning
ID-01Items with no sales history are never auto-ordered, so genuinely new items must be ordered manually the first timeAcceptedBR-09 protects against restocking dead inventory. The cost is a manual first order for new items, which staff already expected to place
ID-02Linked inventory is a fallback, not a sum. If a pack has 1 unit on hand and its linked single has 24, the pack's own count winsAcceptedSumming them would double-count the same physical stock. The fallback is the safer of the two errors
ID-03Missing or zero case quantity silently produces no order for that item rather than an errorAccepted with mitigationBlocking the whole run over one bad master-data row was worse. Mitigation: the item is visible in the calculation detail as zero, which surfaces the master-data gap
ID-04The sales window ends on the newest date in the file, so a stale export produces a stale orderAcceptedDeliberate over using today's date, which would silently understate demand when an export is late. Detectable by staff from the dates on the detail sheet
ID-05Negative inventory is clamped to zero rather than reported as an exceptionKnown gapPrevents bad orders but does not fix the underlying count error. A negative-inventory exception report is the logical next iteration
ID-06No formal before/after stockout measurement was capturedKnown gapSee section 12. The business did not track stockout rates before the change, so the improvement is real but not quantified

11. Rollout and training

Approach

Phased, with the manual process kept available throughout. Nobody was asked to trust generated output before it had been checked against their own.

  1. Parallel run. Orders were generated and compared against staff-prepared orders. Differences were traced to a specific rule, then either the rule was corrected or the difference was accepted with a documented reason.
  2. Rule tuning. Per-vendor window, target days, and inventory credit were adjusted from the defaults where a vendor's ordering pattern justified it.
  3. Review-first cutover. Staff moved to reviewing generated orders, with the manual path still available as a fallback.
  4. Second location. The same engine and rules were extended to the second store, then to the newer export format during the platform migration.

Training and documentation

  • Walkthrough of the review screen and the calculation detail sheet, focused on how to disagree with a number rather than on the tool's features. Staff needed to know what to check, not what to click.
  • Written runbooks for the daily workflow, including what a normal run looks like and how to spot an abnormal one.
  • Known-issue documentation covering the items in section 10, so a surprise had a written explanation.
  • Post-launch support for rule changes as vendors and product mixes changed.

Adoption

The workflow is used daily by everyone at the business who needs it. Ordering has not reverted to the manual process.

12. Results and limitations

Results

OutcomeDetail
Manual order-building time eliminatedRoughly 6 hours per day of assembly replaced by a review step
Daily outputApproximately 20 vendor-ready purchase orders per day, prepared for staff review
Coverage2 locations, roughly 20 vendors, 5,000+ SKUs
Ordering errors reducedStructured, reviewable output replaced manual cross-referencing; pack/single double-ordering addressed by rule rather than by memory

ConsistencyOrdering no longer varies by who is on shift

Limitations, stated plainly

  • No formal baseline was captured. The business did not track stockout rates, order-error rates, or fill rates before the change. Stockouts and missed items visibly decreased, and staff and the owner agree on that, but the improvement is not quantified and should not be presented as a measured percentage.
  • The 6-hour figure is an operational estimate, derived from how long the daily task took staff, not from a time study.
  • Single-business context. The rules encode how ordering works at these two stores. Another retailer would need its own rules, though the structure would transfer.
  • The public demo runs on sanitized synthetic data. It uses the same engine as production, but the demo's catalog, vendor settings, and linked mappings come from bundled fixtures rather than a live database, and no real business data is exposed.
  • Review remains manual by design. Throughput is bounded by staff review time, not by the system. That was the owner's requirement, not a technical limit.

What I would do next

  • Add the negative-inventory exception report described in ID-05, so data errors get surfaced rather than only absorbed.
  • Instrument the baseline that section 12 is missing: capture stockout and order-error counts going forward so the next change can be measured rather than estimated.
  • Add a first-order path for new items so ID-01 stops requiring staff memory.
